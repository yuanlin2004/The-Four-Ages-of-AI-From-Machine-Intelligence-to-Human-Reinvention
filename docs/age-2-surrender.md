# Age II · The Surrender

*The erosion of agency. The question: **who decides?***

<p class="age-window">Illustrative window: 2025–2035? A scenario label, not a forecast.</p>

!!! quote "An illustrative scene"
    Daniel's assistant sends him a year-end summary, as it does every December. He slept better than last year. He spent less and saved more. His blood pressure is down. He was introduced to two people who are now close friends, and he took the job offer the assistant ranked first, which turned out to be the right call.

    At the bottom is a line he has never noticed before: *Decisions made on your behalf this year: 4,312. Decisions you overrode: 9.*

    He tries to remember the last time he chose a restaurant without checking, or planned a trip without asking. He can't. Nothing has gone wrong. Everything is measurably better. He sits with the summary for a long time, trying to work out whether he is a person living a well-run life, or a well-run life that happens to include a person.

## From muscle to judgment

The industrial revolution extended, and in many tasks replaced, human muscle. People still had to decide what the machines should do. Much of the dignity attached to skilled and professional work came to rest on that remaining capacity: judgment, the ability to weigh options and choose well.

AI systems that can recommend, plan and act, the "agents" described in the [technical companion on agents and trust](technical/agents-and-trust.md), reach into that capacity. They do not only answer questions. They choose the route, draft the reply, triage the inbox, rank the candidates, suggest the treatment and, increasingly, carry the choice out.

Each delegation is usually reasonable. The system is often faster, better informed and less tired than we are. The Surrender is not a story about people being forced to give up control. It is a story about people giving it up willingly, one sensible convenience at a time, until the habit and skill of deciding have faded.

**People can keep the formal right to decide while losing the practical capacity to do so.**

## What we already know about automation and skill

The risk is not speculative. Decades of research on automation in aviation, medicine and industry describe the pattern.

- **The ironies of automation.** In 1983 the engineering psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge observed that automating routine work leaves humans with the hardest job of all: taking over, without practice, when the automation fails ([Bainbridge, 1983](references.md#surrender)). The better the system works day to day, the less prepared its human supervisor becomes for the rare day it doesn't.
- **Complacency and automation bias.** Studies of human-automation interaction document "misuse" (over-reliance on automated aids) and a tendency to accept automated recommendations even when contrary evidence is available ([Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010](references.md#surrender)).
- **Skills fade when they are not used.** In one study, people with more lifetime GPS use showed worse spatial memory when navigating without it, and those whose GPS use increased over a three-year follow-up showed a steeper decline ([Dahmani & Bohbot, 2020](references.md#surrender)). The study is correlational in part, but it fits a broad finding that unpracticed abilities weaken.
- **Early signals for generative AI.** In a 2025 survey of knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking, while higher self-confidence was associated with more ([Lee et al., 2025](references.md#surrender)). Self-reports are a weak instrument, and this should be read as a hypothesis worth testing rather than a finding about cognitive decline.

A famous result is worth handling with care: the 2011 "Google effect" study suggested people remember less when they expect information to stay available online ([Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, 2011](references.md#surrender)). The effect tested in a large later replication project did not replicate ([Camerer et al., 2018](references.md#surrender)). The honest summary is that offloading to tools changes what we remember and practice, but the size and permanence of the effect are still debated.

## Defaults are a form of power

Most decisions are not made at all. They are inherited from defaults. A well-known study found that organ-donation consent rates were dramatically higher in countries where donation was the default and people had to opt out than in countries where they had to opt in ([Johnson & Goldstein, 2003](references.md#surrender)). The choice architecture shaped the outcome more than individual preference did ([Thaler & Sunstein, 2008](references.md#surrender)).

A personal AI agent is, among other things, a machine for setting defaults across an entire life. Whoever designs it decides what it optimizes, what it shows, what it hides and what it treats as a "better" outcome. Shoshana Zuboff has argued that digital platforms already gather behavioral data in order to predict and shape behavior for commercial ends ([Zuboff, 2019](references.md#surrender)). An agent that acts on our behalf moves that influence from the feed into the decision itself.

This creates a deep asymmetry. The user experiences one assistant serving one life. The owner of the system sees millions of lives and can tune the defaults for all of them at once.

## Not all delegation is surrender

The most important nuance in this age is that delegation is not inherently a loss. People have always delegated: to doctors, lawyers, elders, priests, accountants and spouses. Good tools have often *expanded* agency. A screen reader gives a blind person independent access to text. A translation tool lets a migrant navigate a bureaucracy alone. A navigation app lets an anxious driver take the trip at all.

Ivan Illich drew a useful line in the 1970s between "convivial" tools, which enlarge what people can do on their own terms, and tools that create dependence and make independent action harder or impossible ([Illich, 1973](references.md#surrender)). The line is not between using a tool and not using it. It is between kinds of reliance.

A practical test follows. Delegation preserves agency when it is:

- **Informed:** the person understands roughly what is being decided and on what basis.
- **Reversible:** they can override, undo or switch off the system without great cost.
- **Contestable:** they can ask why and get an answer, and challenge the outcome.
- **Optional:** a real alternative exists, including doing it themselves.

Delegation becomes surrender when these conditions erode, often without anyone deciding that they should.

## Agency is not only individual

It would be a mistake to frame agency only as a lone individual making choices. In many families and cultures, decisions about marriage, career, money and care have always been made collectively, and that is not a failure of autonomy. The meaningful question is not whether a person decides alone. It is whether the people affected by a decision can still understand it, take part in it and refuse it.

This also moves the concern from personal life to institutions. When public agencies use automated systems to allocate benefits, flag fraud or prioritize cases, the people affected may not know a decision was automated, let alone be able to contest it. The governance of AI decisions, through risk frameworks, audit requirements and rights to explanation, is part of this age's story (see [NIST AI RMF](references.md#surrender)).

!!! info "What would weaken this argument?"
    If long-term studies show that people who rely heavily on AI assistants keep, or even improve, their independent judgment, the Surrender will have been a fear rather than a trend. If agent design converges on transparency, easy override and genuine alternatives, delegation could expand agency on a large scale.

## The question this age leaves us

Daniel's assistant did nothing wrong. Every recommendation really was better by the measures he had agreed to. That is exactly what makes the question hard.

> **When every recommended choice looks better, why, and when, should we still choose for ourselves?**
