# Age IV · The Becoming

*The reinvention of humanity. The question: **what shall we become?***

<p class="age-window">Illustrative window: 2050 and beyond? A scenario label, not a forecast.</p>

!!! quote "An illustrative scene"
    On a hill above the city, an old woman sits with her grandson. Below them, the river is busy with boats no one steers, and the towers are lit by power from plants no one living fully understands.

    The boy has a school assignment: *Interview an elder about their life's work.* He asks his question the way the assignment phrases it. "What was your job, and why was it important?"

    She tells him. She was a pharmacist. Then she watches him try to imagine why anyone would need a person to count pills or check interactions, and she sees the question fall apart in his hands.

    He tries another. "Then what are people *for*?"

    She opens her mouth to say something about work, or contribution, or being useful, and finds that none of those answers fit the world he will grow up in. So she says the only true thing she has. "I don't know yet. I think that's your generation's job." And then, because it is getting cold, "Come here."

## After intelligence stops being the measure

The first three ages describe pressure on three things that many societies used to define a valuable human being: authentic expression, independent judgment and productive contribution. The fourth age begins when that pressure has done its work, when intelligence and productivity no longer reliably distinguish people from machines, and humanity has to decide what will.

This is the age of **becoming**, and the word is chosen carefully. It does not name an outcome. It names an open question that every community will answer differently, with different resources and different degrees of freedom.

It is tempting to define the fourth age by its most spectacular technology, such as machines that discover new mathematics, design new materials or accelerate physics. That possibility is real and is discussed in the [technical companion on AI for science](technical/ai-for-science.md). But scientific acceleration is a catalyst, not a definition. A civilization could make extraordinary discoveries and still have no answer to the boy on the hill.

## Possible paths

The paths below are not predictions. They are possibilities that already have early advocates and early examples. Most likely, several will coexist, in different places and among different groups.

**Leisure and creation.** Keynes imagined that freedom from economic necessity would let people pursue the "art of life itself" ([Keynes, 1930](references.md#becoming)). Some philosophers argue that a world with much less necessary work could be a genuine improvement, if people have the means and culture to fill it with play, craft, learning and relationships ([Danaher, 2019](references.md#becoming)). The risk, visible in the Irrelevance, is that free time without structure or recognition feels like exile rather than freedom.

**Care and community.** Some of what humans value most is valued *because* a human does it: being listened to, nursed, taught or mourned by someone who chose to be there. A society could choose to recognize, fund and honor care, which most economies have long treated as invisible or low-status work.

**New forms of learning.** If education no longer prepares people primarily for employment, it could prepare them for citizenship, judgment, craft and self-understanding. That would be a return, in some ways, to older ideals of education that predate the industrial classroom.

**Deliberate limits.** Communities can decide that some things should stay human, slow or unoptimized. The Amish are often misdescribed as rejecting technology. In fact, many communities evaluate each new technology collectively and adopt it, adapt it or refuse it according to its effect on family and community life ([Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner & Nolt, 2013](references.md#becoming)). In a milder form, France has given many employees a legal "right to disconnect" from work communications outside working hours. The Becoming may produce many such choices, from personal to legal.

**Integration.** Some people will choose deeper integration with AI systems, through constant assistance, augmented cognition or, eventually, direct interfaces. For them, the question "what makes us human?" may be answered by rejecting the line between human and tool. This is a legitimate path, and it should be a choice rather than a requirement for participating in society.

**Concentration.** The same capabilities could support a very different outcome: a small number of firms or states that own the systems and capture most of their benefits, with everyone else dependent on their goodwill. Nothing about the technology prevents this; it depends on ownership, law and politics.

## The darker branch: the Other

There is a version of the fourth age that deserves to be named, even though it is not this essay's prediction. Call it **the Other**: a civilization that humanity still lives inside but no longer understands or meaningfully governs.

In this branch, nothing dramatic happens. No machine rebels. Instead, economic, cultural and political systems that once depended on human participation gradually come to depend on AI systems instead. As they do, they lose the incentives that kept them responsive to human needs. Researchers have described this as **gradual disempowerment**: a loss of human influence that accumulates through many individually reasonable steps, without any single moment of takeover ([Kulveit et al., 2025](references.md#becoming)).

The Other is the shadow of each earlier age. A society that stops recognizing authentic expression, stops exercising judgment and stops being economically necessary might also stop being *politically* necessary, and then find that its preferences carry less and less weight.

The Becoming and the Other are not separate futures. They are the same open moment, seen from the side of agency and from the side of its loss.

## What decides the direction

If the outcome is open, what tips it? The earlier chapters suggest several conditions.

- **Who owns and governs the systems.** Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can successfully govern shared resources through their own institutions, without either full privatization or central control ([Ostrom, 1990](references.md#becoming)). Whether AI infrastructure becomes something like a commons, a utility or a private estate will shape everything else.
- **Whether people can exit and contest.** Delegation that stays informed, reversible, contestable and optional (see [The Surrender](age-2-surrender.md)) preserves the ability to change course.
- **Whether recognition is decoupled from productivity.** Societies that already honor people for kinship, care, faith, citizenship or simply being present may adapt more gracefully than those that have made employment the main source of dignity.
- **Whether the transition is shared.** The fourth age will not arrive everywhere at once. Communities without access to compute, capital or political voice may experience it as something done *to* them.

!!! info "What would weaken this argument?"
    If AI capability plateaus well below the level at which intelligence stops being scarce, the Becoming will remain a thought experiment. If, instead, it arrives quickly and is controlled by a few actors, the "open moment" may close before most people take part in it. Either outcome would make the framing of this chapter wrong in a useful way.

## The question this age leaves us

The grandmother's answer was honest. It may also be the best one available. The point of the fourth age is not that the answer has been found, but that the old answers — *you are what you produce, what you know, what you can do better than others* — no longer work by themselves.

> **When intelligence no longer distinguishes us, what can, and should, define a human life?**
