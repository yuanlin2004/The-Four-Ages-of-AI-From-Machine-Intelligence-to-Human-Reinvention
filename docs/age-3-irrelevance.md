# Age III · The Irrelevance

*The crisis of purpose. The question: **why do we matter?***

<p class="age-window">Illustrative window: 2035–2050? A scenario label, not a forecast.</p>

!!! quote "An illustrative scene"
    Rafael spent thirty years learning how a port breathes: which berths silt up, which captains run late, how a storm in one ocean becomes a backlog in another. He could look at a schedule and feel where it would break.

    The port is busier than ever now, and more profitable. The planning system that replaced his team reroutes ships in seconds and has not had a serious delay in two years. The city is prosperous. His severance was generous. His pension is secure.

    On weekday mornings he walks past the terminal and watches the cranes move without anyone he knows. He is not poor. He is not ill. His grandchildren visit on Sundays. And yet when a neighbor asks what he does, he hears himself say "I used to…" and stops, unsure how to finish a sentence about a man who is no longer needed for anything in particular.

## Usefulness has been the price of dignity

Most societies distribute income through work. Many also distribute something harder to measure through it: status, identity, routine, belonging and the sense of being needed.

The social psychologist Marie Jahoda studied this in the 1930s, when the factory in the Austrian village of Marienthal closed and most of the village lost its jobs. The researchers found not only poverty but a collapse of time and purpose: people lost track of the days, walked more slowly and withdrew from public life ([Jahoda, Lazarsfeld & Zeisel, 1933](references.md#irrelevance)). Jahoda later argued that employment provides "latent" benefits beyond wages, including a structure for time, social contact beyond the family, a share in collective purpose, a recognized status and regular activity ([Jahoda, 1982](references.md#irrelevance)).

More recently, Anne Case and Angus Deaton have linked rising deaths from drugs, alcohol and suicide among Americans without a college degree to the decline of stable working-class jobs and the community life built around them ([Case & Deaton, 2020](references.md#irrelevance)). Michael Sandel argues that societies that tie dignity to credentials and market rewards leave those without them feeling not only poorer but humiliated ([Sandel, 2020](references.md#irrelevance)).

None of this is about AI. It is about how precariously many cultures connect *usefulness* to *worth*. The Irrelevance is the age in which that connection is tested by intelligence that does not need to be paid, trained for decades or persuaded to come to work.

## What AI changes, and what it might not

The technical change behind this age is that cognitive tasks, once performed only by trained people, can increasingly be performed by systems that are cheap to copy and run (see the [technical companions](technical/index.md)).

What the evidence currently shows is mixed and should be stated carefully.

- **Exposure is broad.** One widely cited analysis estimated that about 80% of U.S. workers have at least a tenth of their tasks exposed to large language models, and about 19% have at least half ([Eloundou et al., 2024](references.md#irrelevance)). Exposure is not replacement: an exposed task may be automated, assisted or made more valuable.
- **Productivity effects are real, and uneven in a revealing way.** In a large customer-support deployment, an AI assistant raised the number of issues resolved per hour by about 14–15% on average, with the largest gains for newer and less-skilled workers and little benefit for the most experienced ([Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025](references.md#irrelevance)). In a writing experiment, ChatGPT reduced time spent and raised quality, again compressing the gap between weaker and stronger writers ([Noy & Zhang, 2023](references.md#irrelevance)).

That second finding deserves attention. When a system learns from the work of the best performers and hands their know-how to everyone, the premium on hard-won expertise can fall. That is good news for the novice and for customers. For someone like Rafael, whose standing rested on decades of tacit knowledge, it is the mechanism of irrelevance.

**Not being needed for a task is not the same as being without worth as a person.** The danger of this age is that our institutions, and our own self-understanding, may not know how to tell the difference.

## The case against inevitable irrelevance

A serious version of this chapter must take the counterarguments seriously, because they are strong.

- **New work keeps appearing.** About 60% of U.S. employment in 2018 was in job titles that did not exist in 1940 ([Autor et al., 2024](references.md#irrelevance)). Technology displaces some tasks and creates others; economists describe a race between displacement and the "reinstatement" of labor into new tasks ([Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019](references.md#irrelevance); [Autor, 2015](references.md#irrelevance)).
- **Predictions of imminent displacement are often wrong.** In 2016, a leading AI researcher suggested that training new radiologists was no longer worthwhile. Years later, workforce surveys in the UK still report a shortage of radiologists ([Royal College of Radiologists, 2023](references.md#irrelevance)). Tasks that look automatable in a benchmark are often embedded in judgment, liability, coordination and trust.
- **Some value is in the human doing it.** People pay more to hear a live musician, be cared for by a nurse who knows them, or be taught by someone who has lived what they teach. Care work, in particular, may become more central rather than less.

The argument of this age is not that all work disappears. It is that in *significant domains*, human skill may stop being scarce, and that the social damage of that shift could be large even if total employment holds up, because the jobs that disappear and the jobs that appear may not be held by the same people, in the same places, with the same dignity.

## Who owns the productivity

If AI makes economies far more productive, the question of who receives the gains becomes central. The answer depends on ownership of models, data, compute and capital, on bargaining power and on policy, not on the technology alone.

- **Concentration.** Training frontier models requires enormous capital and energy, which tends to concentrate ownership in a small number of firms and countries.
- **Distribution experiments.** Societies are testing alternatives. In a large U.S. randomized study of a guaranteed income of $1,000 per month, recipients and their partners reduced paid work modestly, by roughly one to two hours a week, mostly in favor of leisure; gains in reported well-being faded after the first year ([Vivalt et al., 2024](references.md#irrelevance)). Such studies say little about a world in which work is scarce. They do suggest two things at once: income can be partly decoupled from employment without collapse, and money alone does not supply the structure and purpose that work provided.
- **Uneven geography.** A task automated in one country may be the export industry of another. Countries whose development strategy relies on outsourced cognitive work, such as call centers, back-office processing and software services, may face this age earlier and harder than the countries where AI is built.

## Worth beyond usefulness is not a new idea

The Irrelevance feels unprecedented in societies where identity is organized around profession. It looks different from elsewhere.

In many communities, status has come primarily from kinship, age, religious role, generosity or service rather than from paid employment. Anthropologists have argued that the centrality of work in modern life is historically specific; James Suzman's history of work, for example, contrasts it with foraging societies in which people often spent fewer hours meeting material needs (a claim that remains debated) ([Suzman, 2020](references.md#irrelevance); [Sahlins, 1972](references.md#irrelevance)).

Hannah Arendt distinguished *labor* (the cyclical effort of sustaining life), *work* (making durable things) and *action* (appearing before others in speech and deed, the political life of a community), and worried that modern society had collapsed all three into labor ([Arendt, 1958](references.md#irrelevance)). David Graeber argued that many people already hold jobs they privately consider pointless, suggesting that employment and meaningful contribution were drifting apart long before AI ([Graeber, 2018](references.md#irrelevance)).

These thinkers do not prove that a world with less necessary work will be a good one. They show that the equation of usefulness with worth is a cultural arrangement, and cultural arrangements can change.

!!! info "What would weaken this argument?"
    If AI mainly augments workers and new tasks appear as fast as old ones vanish, and if gains are broadly shared, the Irrelevance will describe a painful transition for some occupations rather than an age. Evidence to watch: whether the labor share of income falls, whether experienced workers' earnings decline relative to novices', and whether new kinds of work emerge in places where old work disappears.

## The question this age leaves us

In 1930, John Maynard Keynes predicted that within a century, economic growth would make the fifteen-hour working week possible, and that humanity's "permanent problem" would become how to live wisely and well with its freedom ([Keynes, 1930](references.md#irrelevance)). His productivity forecast was broadly right in rich countries. His prediction about work was not, partly because work had come to supply far more than income.

> **If we are no longer economically necessary in familiar ways, where do meaning, recognition and obligation come from?**
